A combo vending machine, one unit dispensing both snacks and drinks, runs roughly $2,000 to $6,195 new, with pricing driven mainly by brand and capacity. A recent-model, cashless-equipped used unit can run as high as $12,500 on the resale market, more than some new machines, because it's paying for a working card reader already installed and provisioned rather than needing a retrofit. Two of the more common manufacturer names in this range are Seaga and AMS, both widely available through vending equipment dealers.

A combo machine trades some capacity in each product category for one machine covering both, which matters most when you're placing equipment somewhere without room or budget for two separate machines. We break down real pricing, capacity, and when combo actually beats running separate snack and drink units.

## What does a combo vending machine actually cost?

Here's the real spread by condition and features.

| Machine condition | Typical price | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| New, entry-level combo (Seaga and similar) | $2,000 to $5,000 | Basic cabinet, standard capacity |
| New, mid-to-higher capacity combo | $3,595 to $6,195 | Larger capacity, more features |
| Used, cashless-equipped, recent model | Up to $12,500 | Priced for the working card reader, not just the cabinet |

A specific data point: the Seaga OVM7 combo model, which vends both beverages and snacks in one cabinet, is commonly listed around $3,979 new, useful as a mid-range anchor price when comparing other listings.

## Is a combo machine actually a good tradeoff versus two separate machines?

Yes when floor space, budget, or a location's traffic level doesn't justify two full machines; no when a location has enough volume in both categories to fill two machines' worth of capacity.

A combo machine puts snacks and drinks in one footprint, which is the entire value proposition: one location fee or floor space allocation instead of two, one service stop instead of two, at the cost of a smaller capacity in each category than a dedicated snack machine and a dedicated drink machine would offer separately. For a small office, waiting room, or lower-traffic site, that tradeoff almost always favors the combo. For a high-traffic location like a large break room or gym, two dedicated machines with full capacity in each category will typically outsell one combo unit that's constantly running low on its best-selling items.

## New or used: which makes more sense for a combo machine?

Used makes sense if you can verify the cashless reader is actually active and the compressor is sound; new makes sense if you want manufacturer warranty coverage and don't want to inspect a used unit yourself.

A used combo machine at $3,595 with a working cashless reader can be a better deal than a new $5,000 unit, but only if that reader is genuinely provisioned with a live processor account, not just physically installed. Ask directly and confirm before buying, since a decorative reader that isn't connected to a payment processor is effectively a cash-only machine at a used-cashless price. New machines remove that uncertainty at a real cost premium, plus they typically come with a manufacturer warranty a used unit won't carry.

## What determines a combo machine's actual capacity?

Cabinet size and the split between snack coils and drink slots, which varies by model and directly trades off against how many total items the machine holds.

A combo machine's total capacity is meaningfully lower than the combined capacity of a dedicated snack machine plus a dedicated drink machine of similar cabinet size, since the combo design splits internal space between two product categories instead of dedicating all of it to one. When comparing combo models, check the actual snack-slot and drink-slot counts rather than just the price, since two combo machines at the same price point can have very different real-world capacity.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does a new combo vending machine cost?**
New combo machines typically run $2,000 to $6,195 depending on brand and capacity, with a mid-range example like the Seaga OVM7 commonly listed around $3,979.

**Is a used combo vending machine a good deal?**
It can be, especially if it has a working cashless reader, since used cashless-equipped combo units can run up to $12,500 but still cost less than replacing a cash-only machine and retrofitting a reader separately. Confirm the reader is actively provisioned with a payment processor, not just physically installed, before buying.

**Should I buy a combo machine or two separate snack and drink machines?**
A combo machine makes sense for lower-traffic locations or sites with limited floor space, since it covers both categories in one footprint. High-traffic locations usually do better with two dedicated machines, since each holds significantly more capacity in its category than a combo unit splits between both.

**What brands make combo vending machines?**
Seaga and AMS are two of the more commonly available combo machine manufacturers sold through vending equipment dealers, with new units from both brands typically falling in the $2,000 to $6,195 range depending on model and capacity.
