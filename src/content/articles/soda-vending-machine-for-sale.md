A new soda vending machine for sale runs $2,950 for a basic slim combo unit to $14,500 for a large advertising-screen combo, with most standard drink or combo machines landing between $3,650 and $8,650. Refurbished machines from a reseller typically cost 30% to 50% less than the equivalent new unit, while private-party used listings on eBay or Craigslist run under $300 to $600 for older, unwarrantied units. The number on the price tag is only part of the decision: whether you buy a branded (Pepsi, Coca-Cola) or unbranded machine changes what you're legally allowed to stock inside it.

This guide covers what actually drives soda machine pricing, the brand-lock issue most first-time buyers miss, and how to tell a genuinely good used listing from a risky one.

## Does a Pepsi or Coke-branded machine restrict what you can sell?

Often yes: a branded soda machine acquired through a bottler placement agreement typically comes with a supply contract that restricts you to that brand's products, not a free-standing asset you stock however you want.

This is the single most overlooked issue in soda machine buying. A machine with Pepsi or Coca-Cola graphics wrapped on the front isn't automatically yours to fill with whatever you want. If the machine came from a bottler placement program, you may be contractually required to buy product exclusively from that bottler at their wholesale pricing, which can be less flexible (and sometimes less profitable per can) than an unbranded machine you stock from a wholesale club or distributor of your choosing. Before buying a branded machine secondhand, confirm in writing whether any bottler supply agreement transfers with the machine or terminates at sale. An unbranded machine, sometimes called a "generic" combo or drink unit, has no such restriction and is the simpler choice for a new independent operator.

## Bottle, can, or both: which capacity actually matters?

Can-focused machines fit more units per column and suit high-volume, price-sensitive locations; bottle capability matters more where customers expect 16 to 20-ounce bottled sizes, like gyms and larger offices.

Most modern combo and drink machines vend both cans and bottles from adjustable-width columns, so the practical decision is less "can versus bottle" and more total capacity and column count for your specific product mix. A machine with 8 to 10 columns gives you room for multiple soda flavors plus water and a sports drink or two, which outperforms a 5-column machine stocked with only soda in most break room and gym locations, since customers appreciate having a water or diet option even in a soda-focused machine.

## Is a used or refurbished soda machine actually a good deal?

Usually yes if it comes from a reseller with a parts warranty, since refrigerated soda machines carry real compressor risk that's hard to assess without testing.

A refurbished unit from a reputable reseller commonly includes a 90-day parts warranty and prior inspection, which meaningfully de-risks the purchase compared to a private-party listing with no service history. The refrigeration compressor is the component most likely to fail on an older soda machine, and a failed compressor can cost $200 to $500 to replace, see our repair cost breakdown, which can erase most of the savings from a cheap private-party deal. If you're buying from an individual seller rather than a dealer, budget that repair risk into your offer price rather than assuming the sticker price is the real cost.

## Snippet-ready: soda machine buying checklist

1. Confirm branded versus unbranded status and whether any bottler supply contract transfers with the machine.
2. Test the compressor by running the machine for at least an hour before buying, refrigeration is the highest-risk component on any used soda machine.
3. Check cashless payment compatibility. Cash-only soda machines are losing sales to modern combo units at a growing share of locations.
4. Confirm column count and adjustable width match your intended product mix (soda, water, sports drinks).
5. Ask for a parts warranty in writing if buying refurbished; walk away from private-party listings with zero service history and a price that seems too low.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much does a soda vending machine cost?**
New machines run $2,950 to $14,500 depending on size and features, with most standard combo units landing between $3,650 and $8,650. Refurbished machines typically cost 30% to 50% less than the new equivalent, and private-party used listings can run under $300 to $600, though without a warranty.

**Can I put any soda I want in a Pepsi or Coke vending machine?**
Only if there's no bottler supply agreement attached to the machine. Machines placed through a branded bottler program often come with a contract requiring you to buy that brand's product exclusively. Confirm this in writing before buying a branded machine secondhand.

**Is it cheaper to buy a used soda machine than new?**
Yes, typically 30% to 50% cheaper through a reseller, or significantly less through a private-party listing, but the refrigeration compressor is the main risk on any used soda machine, so a dealer-refurbished unit with a warranty is the safer used option.

**Do soda vending machines need special electrical service?**
Most standard soda and combo machines run on standard 120V household-grade outlets, similar to a residential refrigerator. Larger elevator or advertising-screen combo units may draw more power, so check the specific model's electrical spec before installing.

**What's the difference between a soda machine and a combo machine?**
A dedicated soda or drink machine only vends beverages. A combo machine vends both snacks and drinks from the same unit, which is more common in most standard locations since it serves more of a location's demand from one machine footprint.
