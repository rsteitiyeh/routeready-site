A single-head bulk vending machine (gumball, candy, or toy capsule) for sale costs $80 to $150 new from a specialty dealer, a triple-head unit runs $215 to $350, and a multi-head vending rack with 6 to 8 heads runs $550 to $1,600. Electronic, card-capable bulk vendors cost more, typically $1,500 to $4,800. That's the equipment side. The route economics matter more than the machine price: the national average is roughly $1 per machine per day, or about $30 a month, but a genuinely well-placed machine in a busy location can generate $400 to $700 or more.

Bulk vending is the lowest-cost entry point into the vending industry by a wide margin, which is exactly why the gap between a mediocre location and a great one matters so much more here than in full-size snack and drink vending.

## How much does a bulk vending machine actually earn per month?

Nationally, gumball and candy machines average about $1 a day, or roughly $30 a month, but that number hides an enormous range: some machines don't clear $2 a month, while a genuinely well-placed machine can generate $400 to $700 or more.

Product cost runs low: a gumball or piece of bulk candy typically costs $0.02 to $0.05 wholesale and sells for $0.25 to $0.50, a gross margin most other retail categories can't touch. Location traffic, not machine features, is what turns that margin into real income. A machine in a busy family restaurant waiting area or a bowling alley routinely outperforms an identical machine in a quiet office hallway by a wide multiple, because bulk vending purchases are almost entirely impulse buys tied to foot traffic and dwell time, not planned purchases.

## What commission do locations expect for hosting a bulk machine?

Most locations expect 10% to 25% of gross revenue, though some higher-traffic or exclusive placements command 20% to 40%.

Because product cost is so low, even a generous commission split still leaves the operator with the majority of revenue: an operator offering a location 20% to 40% commission typically still keeps 57% to 77% of gross sales after candy cost and commission. That math is unusually favorable compared to full-size snack and drink vending, where commission and product cost together often eat a larger share of revenue. Negotiate commission based on the location's actual traffic, not a flat number you apply everywhere. A slow location doesn't deserve the same 25% you'd offer a high-traffic one.

## Which locations actually work for a bulk vending route?

Restaurants and bars with waiting areas, bowling alleys and skating rinks, laundromats, gyms, and any retail space with steady walk-in traffic and a captive few minutes of dwell time.

The common thread across strong bulk vending locations is dwell time, a customer standing or waiting somewhere with nothing else to do for a few minutes. That's why restaurant waiting areas, laundromats, and auto repair shop lobbies consistently outperform locations with fast in-and-out traffic like a gas station counter. Schools, colleges, and youth-focused locations (Boys and Girls Clubs, skating rinks) also perform well specifically because bulk candy and toy capsules skew toward a younger buyer.
